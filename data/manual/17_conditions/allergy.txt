title: Drug allergy
[i]A reaction to lidocaine, morphine or propofol, named on the chart or not.[/i]

[b]On the chart:[/b] “Allergic to lidocaine.”, “Allergic to morphine.”, “Allergic to propofol.” or “Claims a drug allergy but forgot which one.”

[b]Diagnosis[/b]
• A named allergy needs no test: avoid the drug and use an alternative from its page below.
• An unnamed one: order the allergy screen at the monitor ([b]Order blood work[/b]). The result shows on the monitor after {Lab.Panels.allergy.Seconds} seconds. The full panel includes it but takes {Lab.Panels.full.Seconds}. The laboratory takes no new order for {Lab.Cooldown} seconds after each one.
• The patient may be mistaken: the screen can come back with nothing found.

[b]While the agent is unknown[/b]
• Give none of lidocaine, morphine or propofol until the result is back.
• Where the procedure can't wait, use an agent that is never an allergen: ketamine or the inhaled agent.
• [b]Warning:[/b] the alternatives are stronger and carry hazards of their own. Use one only when the allergy calls for it. Given to a patient who could have had the usual drug, it counts against the surgeon.

[b]Reaction[/b]
• Nothing shows on the skin. Watch the monitor: systolic pressure falls by {Patient.AllergyPressureDrop} mmHg at once and by up to {Patient.AllergySwelling * Patient.SwellingPressure} mmHg more while the swelling lasts. Oxygen saturation falls by up to {Patient.AllergySwelling * Patient.SwellingSpo2}%.
• Untreated, the swelling goes down over {Patient.AllergySwelling / Patient.SwellingFade} seconds.
• More swelling on top of it (a second reaction, a transfusion reaction) is anaphylaxis: the heart is at risk of arrest.

[b]Treatment[/b]
• Stop giving the drug.
• Diphenhydramine takes the swelling down in about {Patient.AllergySwelling / (Patient.SwellingFade + Patient.AntihistamineFade)} seconds.
• Adrenaline works faster still and raises the pressure: give it for anaphylaxis or when the pressure alarm sounds.
> See Combined conditions (14.5, 14.6).
